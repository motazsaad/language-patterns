One small word, different rules

Does “the” have gender?

Some languages change the definite article to agree with a noun’s grammatical gender; others keep it the same.

Language Gender of nouns Forms of “the” Examples
English Common nouns have no grammatical gender. the for all nouns. the book; the table
French Nouns are masculine or feminine. le (masculine), la (feminine); les (plural). le livre (book); la table (table)
Italian Nouns are masculine or feminine. Forms vary by gender, number, and following sound: il / lo / la / l’; plural i / gli / le. il libro (book); la casa (house)
Arabic Nouns are generally masculine or feminine. الـ (al-) is the same written form for both. الْكِتَاب (al-kitāb, the book); السَّيَّارَة (as-sayyāra, the car)

↳ Arabic still has grammatical gender: the noun (and words agreeing with it) carries that information, so the article itself does not change for masculine and feminine. Before some Arabic consonants, the article’s l sound assimilates in speech, as in as-sayyāra; its written form remains الـ.

Gender can change from language to language

A noun’s grammatical gender is a feature of that language—not a property shared by the thing itself. Adjectives and articles may agree with the noun.

Meaning English French Italian Arabic
flower No grammatical gender la fleur — feminine il fiore — masculine زَهْرَة — zahra, feminine
street No grammatical gender la rue — feminine la strada — feminine شارِع — shāriʿ, masculine
a white flower white flower une fleur blanche un fiore bianco زَهْرَة بَيْضاء — zahra bayḍāʾ, “a white flower”

↳ The flower is feminine in French and Arabic but masculine in Italian. The street is feminine in French and Italian but masculine in Arabic. In “white flower,” the adjective changes to agree with the noun in French, Italian, and Arabic; English adjectives do not change.

Reflexive verbs: doing something to yourself

French and Italian commonly use a short pronoun with reflexive verbs. But the same idea does not always use a reflexive verb in every language: English may use an ordinary verb or “myself,” and Arabic often uses a different verb or construction.

Meaning English French Italian Arabic
wash oneself I wash myself. Je me lave. Mi lavo. أَغْسِلُ نَفْسِي — aghsilu nafsī, “I wash myself”
stand I stand. Je me tiens debout. Sto in piedi. أَقِفُ — aqifu, “I stand”
get up / stand up I get up. Je me lève. Mi alzo. أَنْهَضُ — anhaḍu, “I get up”
remember I remember. Je me souviens. Mi ricordo. أَتَذَكَّرُ — atadhakkaru, “I remember”
feel tired I feel tired. Je me sens fatigué(e). Mi sento stanco/a. أَشْعُرُ بِالتَّعَبِ — ashʿuru bit-taʿabi, “I feel tiredness”

↳ French se lever, se souvenir, and se sentir, and Italian alzarsi, ricordarsi, and sentirsi are reflexive in these examples. Their English and Arabic equivalents are not necessarily reflexive. Arabic also has أَغْتَسِلُ (aghtasilu, “I bathe / wash myself”).

Negation takes different shapes

Even the simple idea “I do not understand” is built differently.

English French Italian Arabic
I do not understand. Je ne comprends pas. Non capisco. لا أَفْهَمُ — lā afhamu, “I do not understand”

↳ English uses do not + verb; standard French places ne … pas around the verb (often just pas in casual speech); Italian puts non before it. Standard Arabic uses lā before the present-tense verb. Arabic negation also varies with tense and dialect.

Conjugation: endings, helpers, and roots

Conjugation changes a verb to show things like person, number, and tense. English uses a relatively small set of verb endings and often relies on helper words; French and Italian have more distinct endings.

Language How verbs show who is acting Example: “I write”
English Few verb endings; pronouns and helper verbs do much of the work. I write / she writes
French Verb endings vary by person; subject pronouns are usually said. j’écris
Italian Verb endings vary by person and often make the subject pronoun optional. scrivo (“I write”)
Arabic Prefixes and suffixes mark person, number, and gender; many words are built from consonant roots in patterns. أَكْتُبُ — aktubu, “I write”

↳ A common Arabic root is ك-ت-ب (k-t-b), associated with writing. Patterns add vowels and sometimes extra consonants: كَتَبَ (kataba, “he wrote”), كاتِب (kātib, “writer”), and كِتاب (kitāb, “book”). Root-and-pattern is a useful guide, though not every word is formed in a perfectly predictable way.

A root can also build a word for a place

A common place-noun pattern starts with مـ (m-) and fits the root into a template. Some place nouns use a feminine ending ـة (-a); the exact pattern depends on the word.

Root & idea Verb example Place noun Pattern / meaning
ك-ت-ب (k-t-b, writing) كَتَبَ (kataba, “he wrote”); أَكْتُبُ (aktubu, “I write”) مَكْتَب (maktab, office / desk) مَفْعَل (mafʿal), masculine
د-ر-س (d-r-s, studying) دَرَسَ (darasa, “he studied”); أَدْرُسُ (adrusu, “I study”) مَدْرَسَة (madrasa, school) مَفْعَلَة (mafʿala), feminine ending -a
ل-ع-ب (l-ʿ-b, playing) لَعِبَ (laʿiba, “he played”); أَلْعَبُ (alʿabu, “I play”) مَلْعَب (malʿab, playing field / stadium) مَفْعَل (mafʿal), masculine

↳ Think of the root consonants as the core idea and the pattern as a template. For instance, the m- place pattern appears in maktab and malʿab; madrasa uses a feminine pattern ending in ة (-a). This is a useful pattern, not a rule that every place name is made by simply adding m and tāʾ marbūṭa.

The letter C—and the Arabic letter ج

The Latin letter C is named differently in English, French, and Italian, and its sound in a word depends on the language and what follows it. Arabic script has no C. The distinct Arabic letter ج is called jīm; it is not the Arabic alphabet’s third letter.

Language Letter name / sound Example or note
English C is named “see” (/siː/). c is /k/ in cat and /s/ in city.
French C is named cé (/se/). Usually /k/ before a, o, u; /s/ before e, i, y.
Italian C is named ci (/tʃi/). Usually /k/ before a, o, u; /tʃ/ (like “ch” in cheese) before e, i.
Arabic No C; ج is jīm. Often /dʒ/ in Modern Standard Arabic; pronunciation varies by dialect, including /g/ in Egyptian Arabic and /ʒ/ in some Levantine varieties.
